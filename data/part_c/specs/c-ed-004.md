- 71-year-old man with known heart failure, emergency department visit.
- Three days of increasing shortness of breath on exertion and swelling of both legs. He sleeps on three pillows. No chest pain, no fever, no cough.
- An echocardiogram last month showed an ejection fraction of 30 percent.
- He takes furosemide and carvedilol every day and has not missed doses.
- No history of high blood pressure, diabetes or kidney disease.
- Examination: temperature 36.8 C, heart rate 96 and regular, blood pressure 118/70, respiratory rate 22, oxygen saturation 93 percent on room air, neck veins distended, crackles at both lung bases, pitting edema of both legs to the knees.
- Chest X-ray read by the emergency physician: pulmonary vascular congestion, no infiltrate. Electrocardiogram: sinus rhythm, no acute changes. Troponin normal, B-type natriuretic peptide elevated, creatinine normal.
- Treatment: intravenous furosemide with good urine output and easier breathing; oxygen saturation rose to 96 percent on room air.
- Diagnosis in the emergency physician's words: acute on chronic heart failure.
- The physician does not state the type of heart failure. The words systolic, diastolic, reduced ejection fraction and preserved ejection fraction do not appear in the note; only the number does.
- Discharged with a higher dose of furosemide, cardiology clinic visit in three days.
